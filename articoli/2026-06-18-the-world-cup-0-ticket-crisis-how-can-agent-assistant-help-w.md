# The World Cup “$0 Ticket” Crisis: How Can Agent Assistant Help With Crisis Customer Service

In early June, FIFA accidentally gave away about 60 World Cup tickets for free due to a payment glitch on its official website. The tickets were for group-stage matches in Toronto. Instead of owning the mistake, FIFA gave affected fans seven days to pay the full price — or lose their seats.

 

That wasn't the only problem. One fan paid $1,600 for four tickets but only one showed up in his app. Another spent $3,800 on Category 2 tickets and ended up near the stadium roof — while better seats in the same category were listed for $800 less. Fans reported spending a full week calling and emailing just to reach a human being.

 

The backlash was severe. Attorneys general from New York, New Jersey, and California launched investigations into FIFA's ticketing practices. New Jersey's AG put it bluntly: FIFA turned buying tickets into "a gauntlet of confusion, fake scarcity, and impossibly high prices".

## Why crisis response separates good brands from bad ones

Technical failures happen. Payment glitches, system errors, miscommunications — they're inevitable at scale. What separates trustworthy brands from the rest is how they respond when things go wrong.

 

FIFA's response created a PR nightmare because their customer service wasn't equipped for the crisis. Fans couldn't reach anyone. Agents had no consistent information. Policies changed by the hour. The result? Angry customers, regulatory investigations, and lasting reputational damage.

## What crisis-ready support looks like

When volume spikes and information changes fast, frontline agents need three things: real-time access to the latest policies, automated workflows to reduce manual errors, and intelligent guidance to handle emotional conversations. Without these, even the best agents fall apart under pressure.

 

The right AI co-pilot transforms crisis response by doing exactly that — surfacing approved responses, auto-filling forms, monitoring sentiment, and ing supervisors when things go off track. The result? Faster resolution, consistent messaging, and fewer costly mistakes.
